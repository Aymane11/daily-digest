Cerebras files to raise $3.5B in IPO at $26.6B valuation, targets Nvidia on inference speed

The AI chipmaker's Wafer-Scale Engine 3 — 900,000 cores, 44 GB on-chip memory — claims faster and cheaper inference than GPU alternatives. A $10B+ deal with OpenAI and an AWS partnership underpin the offering.

¶01 WHAT HAPPENED

Cerebras Systems filed updated IPO paperwork on 4 May 2026, planning to sell 28 million shares at $115–$125 on the Nasdaq. At the high end, the offering values the AI chip company at $26.6B and raises $3.5B — making it the largest tech IPO so far this year.

The company's Wafer-Scale Engine 3 (WSE-3) is the world's largest commercial chip, combining 900,000 compute cores and 44 GB of on-chip SRAM — replacing the high-bandwidth memory (HBM) stacks that GPUs rely on — with 21 petabytes of memory bandwidth. Cerebras claims WSE-3 is faster for inference tasks while consuming less power than comparable Nvidia configurations.

Revenue grew 76% to $510M in 2025, with net income of $237.8M. The business has pivoted from selling chips outright to a cloud service model, which provides more predictable recurring revenue and locks in long-term compute contracts. A reported $10B+ multi-year deal with OpenAI (providing up to 750 MW of capacity through 2028) and a recently announced AWS partnership give the IPO a credible demand signal.

The offering is planned for mid-May. If it prices at or above the high end, it will be the largest tech IPO of 2026 by a significant margin.

¶02 WHY IT MATTERS

The Cerebras IPO is the clearest signal yet that AI infrastructure — not AI applications — is where institutional capital wants to park money in 2026. The chip layer, inference engines, and compute contracts command higher multiples than software built on top of them, and Cerebras is betting that on-chip memory bandwidth is the constraint that WSE-3 solves better than any GPU configuration.

The risk is concentration. The OpenAI deal represents a majority of Cerebras revenue, and OpenAI is itself a customer of Nvidia. If OpenAI's workloads shift back to GPU clusters — or if OpenAI develops its own silicon (rumored for 2027) — Cerebras faces a cliff.

¶03 WHAT TO WATCH
  • Check the S-1 filing for customer concentration numbers — if OpenAI represents more than 50% of revenue, the IPO risk profile shifts dramatically. The SEC filing at sec.gov contains the exact figures.
  • Watch the pricing date in mid-May: if the deal prices at the high end ($125), it signals institutional confidence in non-Nvidia AI infrastructure. If it prices below $115 or is delayed, read it as skepticism about the OpenAI dependency.
  • Evaluate whether Cerebras is available on AWS as a drop-in alternative for your inference workloads — the AWS partnership means you may be able to run benchmarks without a procurement agreement.
