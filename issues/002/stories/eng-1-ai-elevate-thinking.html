ENGINEERING CAREER

AI should elevate your thinking, not replace it

TL;DR — Koshy John argues that AI creates a critical skill divide: engineers who use it to think deeper build compounding advantage; those who use it to avoid thinking build a hollow foundation that fails under pressure. The piece is grounded in a single testable question: can you defend the reasoning behind your last AI-assisted decision without the AI in the room?

MON 27 APR 2026 · 6 MIN READ · SOURCE: KOSHYJOHN.COM · HN 501 PTS

¶01 — The argument

John opens with an observation that AI has created a surface-level productivity that is impossible to distinguish from genuine capability unless you probe it. Engineers can present AI-generated design decisions, architecture diagrams, and code reviews that look polished and authoritative. The problem: they cannot reproduce the reasoning, defend the tradeoffs, or spot when the AI was wrong.

“People can present machine-produced reasoning they do not understand, cannot defend, and could not reproduce on their own”—what John calls intellectual dependency being labeled as leverage. It can look like talent. It is a dead end.

The calculator analogy is precise here: a calculator enhances the skill of someone who already understands arithmetic. Used before that understanding exists, it prevents the skill from forming. The same distinction applies to LLMs and software engineering judgment.

¶02 — What to delegate and what to own

John draws a clear line. Mechanical work—boilerplate, test scaffolding, summarization, format conversion—is exactly what AI should handle. The reclaimed time should go to higher-order thinking, not to more busywork. But there is a set of activities that must remain under human ownership:

CONCRETE RECOMMENDATIONS

  1. Own problem framing. Before prompting, write down in your own words what the problem actually is and what constraints matter. Never let the AI define the problem space.
  2. Own tradeoff analysis. After receiving AI output, enumerate the alternatives the AI did not present and the costs it ignored. Treat AI output as a first draft, not a decision.
  3. Own risk identification. Ask “what could go wrong with this” yourself before asking the AI. Compare your list to the AI’s list. The delta is where you are building or losing judgment.
  4. Delegate boilerplate, scaffolding, and format work. These are the clearest wins—high volume, low judgment, well-defined output.
  5. Review AI output as you would review a junior engineer’s PR. Not as a formality—with genuine skepticism about whether the reasoning holds up.

¶03 — The leadership angle

John’s warning for leaders is the organizational mirror of the individual risk. When engineers use AI to simulate comprehension rather than build it, code reviews get weaker, design discussions get shallower, and documents become more polished and less useful. The degradation is invisible on a sprint-by-sprint basis and obvious only when something breaks under pressure.

For evaluation: test reasoning ability, not output quality. The tell is asking someone to explain why they made a decision, not just what the decision was. Polished fluency and genuine understanding look identical at the output level; they diverge immediately under questioning.

For early-career engineers specifically, John is direct: skipping the struggle of building foundational skills to appear productive creates long-term damage that is hard to recover from. The skills are built through friction—through getting things wrong and fixing them. No shortcut preserves that.

AUDIT — ANSWER HONESTLY

QUICK START — DO THIS TODAY (<30 MIN)

Pick one AI-assisted decision from the past week. Write a single paragraph explaining the tradeoffs in your own words—without opening any chat window. If you get stuck, that gap is exactly where your judgment needs work. Then go back and compare what you wrote to what the AI said. Notice what you missed and what the AI missed.

Module 06 — Why this matters for your career

Mental model: Think of AI as a power tool. A skilled carpenter with a nail gun builds faster and better than a skilled carpenter with a hammer. A beginner with a nail gun builds faster than a beginner with a hammer but slower than either skilled carpenter—and they skip the learning that makes them skilled. The power tool compounds skill; it does not replace the prerequisite.

Bring it to your team: At your next architecture review, spend 10 minutes before the meeting writing down every risk and tradeoff you can identify independently. Then compare with whatever AI-assisted preparation the presenter did. Facilitate a discussion about the delta—what your collective human judgment surfaced that the AI did not, and vice versa. Make this a standing practice.

30-day experiment: For one month, log every AI-assisted judgment call you make (architecture decisions, code review comments, design choices). Weekly, pick one and try to reconstruct the reasoning from scratch without the AI. Track whether your reconstruction quality improves. If it does, you are building judgment. If it stays flat or worsens, you are becoming dependent.

THE DIVIDE

LEVERAGE

Uses AI to eliminate mechanical work. Owns judgment, framing, risk. Builds faster and deeper simultaneously.

DEPENDENCY

Uses AI to avoid judgment. Output looks polished. Cannot defend reasoning. Skills atrophy under the appearance of productivity.

THE TEST

“Can you defend the reasoning behind your last AI-assisted decision without the AI in the room?”
