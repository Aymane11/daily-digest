Inside Lambda networking: how AWS shrunk VPC cold starts from 150ms to 200μs

Werner Vogels details how the Lambda team used eBPF, constant-work patterns, and per-namespace isolation to solve the decade-old VPC cold start problem

BY · EDITORIAL TEAM · 7 MIN · ENGINEERING · DEPTH

AWS CTO Werner Vogels published a deep-dive into the invisible engineering behind Lambda's VPC networking. The team solved the VPC cold start problem — where setting up a Geneve tunnel could take 150ms — by moving expensive tunnel creation off the hot path using eBPF. The result: tunnel setup dropped from 150ms to 200μs, a 750x improvement.

05 WHAT IT MEANS
3 TAKEAWAYS · LINKS
01

N+1 scaling costs are everywhere — and fixable

The Lambda team found that tunnel creation, rule evaluation, and lock contention all scaled with slot density. The fix was to decouple setup from invocation. Audit your system for work that grows per-request but could be pre-computed at boot.

02

Namespace isolation beats monolithic rule management

By moving per-slot iptables rules into individual network namespaces, Lambda eliminated rule explosion (125K→144) and performance skew between hot and cold slots. Apply this pattern to configuration, routing, and permissions.

03

eBPF is the right tool for middlebox replacement

Lambda replaced stateful NAT (conntrack) with stateless eBPF programs, achieving 100x improvement. Evaluate whether your network functions (NAT, filtering, load balancing) can become stateless eBPF/XDP programs.

06 WHY THIS MATTERS FOR YOUR CAREER
MENTAL MODEL · TEAM · EXPERIMENT

THE MENTAL MODEL

Shift from incremental to constant work

The most important idea in this article is not eBPF or iptables — it's asking "what work can we do exactly once per worker, rather than once per invocation?" This is a universal systems design pattern. Most performance problems come from paying N times for something that could be paid once. Internalize the question: "If I pre-compute this at boot or deploy time, can I eliminate it from the hot path entirely?"

BRING IT TO YOUR TEAM

In your next architecture review, pick one service and ask: "Map every operation this service performs per-request. Which ones could we do once per deploy instead? What would it take to front-load that cost?" Don't ask for estimates — ask for a list. The Lambda team started with a list and found tunnel creation, iptables evaluation, and lock contention. Your list might reveal database connection setup, configuration parsing, or cache warming — all solvable with the constant-work pattern.

THE 30-DAY EXPERIMENT

Week 1: Pick one service in your production stack. Run `iptables-save | wc -l`, `conntrack -C`, and measure p99 cold-start or first-request latency. Write down the current numbers.

Week 2: Identify the top three per-request setup operations.

Week 3: Prototype moving one of them to boot time (pre-warm connections, pre-compute routing tables, shift rules into namespaces).

Week 4: Measure p99 again and compare. Share the delta with your team — the number is the argument.

CONCRETE · RECOMMENDATIONS

1. Audit your system's N+1 scaling costs — map every per-request operation and identify which ones scale linearly with density

2. Evaluate eBPF for any stateful network function in your stack — start with `bpftrace` one-liners to measure kernel overhead

3. Isolate per-tenant configuration into separate namespaces (network, cgroup, mount) — eliminate shared rule bloat

4. Adopt the constant-work pattern: move setup operations to boot/deploy time, not request time

RADAR · CHECKLIST

Do you know how many iptables/nftables rules apply to each request?

Does your system have per-request overhead that grows with tenant count?

QUICK START

Try this now: run iptables-save | wc -l on one production instance and conntrack -C on the same machine. Then pick a microservice and trace its full request path — what happens between socket accept and response write. Identify three things that would scale poorly at 10x the current request rate. Write a one-paragraph plan for each. Total time: 25 minutes.
