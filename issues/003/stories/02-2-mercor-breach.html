SECURITY SUPPLY CHAIN BIOMETRICS

4TB of voice prints and ID documents stolen from 40,000 AI contractors in a LiteLLM supply chain attack on Mercor

Lapsus$ exploited a malicious LiteLLM release (95M monthly downloads) to harvest credentials and pivot into Mercor’s systems. Five lawsuits filed in ten days. Organizations pinning dependencies with cryptographic hashes were fully protected.

BY · EDITORIAL TEAM · 5 MIN

★ WHAT HAPPENED

In late February 2026, threat group TeamPCP compromised Trivy—an open-source security scanner—by stealing maintainer credentials via a GitHub Actions vulnerability. On March 24, they published two malicious LiteLLM versions (1.82.7 and 1.82.8) to PyPI within 13 minutes of each other. LiteLLM, downloaded 95 million times monthly, holds API keys for dozens of AI providers. The malicious payload harvested credentials, exfiltrated data, and installed persistent backdoors. Mercor—a $10B AI training startup serving OpenAI, Anthropic, and Meta—ran the compromised library. The breach exposed 4TB of data: studio-quality voice recordings averaging two to five minutes per contractor, government-issued ID documents, Social Security numbers, face biometrics, video interviews, and source code. On April 4, Lapsus$ posted the archive on its leak site. Five contractor lawsuits were filed within ten days.

¶ 01 WHY · IT · MATTERS

Voice prints paired with verified identity documents enable a complete attacker toolkit: bank verification bypasses, deepfake video calls, vishing campaigns against employers, insurance fraud, and scams targeting elderly victims. The combination is uniquely dangerous because voice was collected under a “training data” framing—contractors did not understand they were generating permanent biometric identifiers. The broader implication: LiteLLM’s architectural role (it holds API keys for multiple AI providers) made it an exceptionally high-value target. Thousands of companies that used the library were potentially exposed; Mercor is simply the most visible victim. Delve Technologies, the GRC firm that had SOC 2–certified LiteLLM, was simultaneously exposed as running fabricated compliance reports—99.8% identical text across 494 clients. Delve was expelled from Y Combinator on April 4.

¶ 02 WHAT · TO · WATCH

Audit your requirements.txt or pyproject.toml today: check whether you pin LiteLLM and its dependencies with cryptographic hashes (use pip-compile --generate-hashes or switch to uv.lock). Organizations with locked dependency files were fully protected. Evaluate whether any service in your stack holds AI provider credentials in an environment variable accessible to a library like LiteLLM—consider using a secrets manager with tight IAM scoping instead. Watch for NSA/CISA/FBI joint guidance on AI supply chain security expected before EU AI Act enforcement in August 2026.
