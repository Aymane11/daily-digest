SECURITY SUPPLY CHAIN

The LiteLLM supply chain attack hit thousands of companies—teams using locked dependency files with cryptographic hashes were fully protected

Attackers poisoned two PyPI versions in 13 minutes via a compromised Trivy maintainer account. LiteLLM holds AI provider API keys for every company that uses it—making it uniquely attractive. A compliance firm certifying it was simultaneously exposed as fabricating SOC 2 reports.

BY · EDITORIAL TEAM · 2 MIN

★ WHAT HAPPENED

In February 2026, TeamPCP exploited a GitHub Actions vulnerability in Trivy (an open-source security scanner) to steal maintainer credentials. On March 24, they used those credentials to publish two malicious LiteLLM versions (1.82.7 and 1.82.8) to PyPI within 13 minutes. LiteLLM is downloaded 95 million times monthly and typically holds API keys for multiple AI providers in its runtime environment. The malicious payload ran in three stages: credential harvesting, data exfiltration, and backdoor installation. Thousands of companies were affected. Delve Technologies, the GRC firm that had issued a SOC 2 certification for LiteLLM, was simultaneously exposed: analysis of 494 leaked SOC 2 reports showed 99.8% identical text across all clients. Delve was expelled from Y Combinator on April 4.

¶ 01 WHY · IT · MATTERS

This attack exposed the single biggest structural risk in AI development supply chains: libraries that aggregate AI provider credentials are exceptionally high-value targets. The Delve scandal compounds the problem—SOC 2 certifications, the primary third-party security validation most teams rely on, can be fabricated. Both findings together mean that the standard AI development trust stack (certified dependency, managed API key, production deployment) has a demonstrated gap that requires teams to implement their own verification rather than outsourcing trust to third-party certifiers.

¶ 02 WHAT · TO · WATCH

Audit your Python dependency files today: switch to uv.lock or run pip-compile --generate-hashes to pin all transitive dependencies with cryptographic hashes—this is the one control that would have prevented this attack entirely. Check whether any service in your stack runs LiteLLM versions 1.82.7 or 1.82.8; if so, treat all credentials in that environment as compromised and rotate them. Watch for OWASP AI Bill of Materials (AIBOM) tooling to mature as a standard response to this class of attack.
