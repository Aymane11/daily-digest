The Next Two Years of Software Engineering

Addy Osmani maps the five questions that will determine which engineers thrive when AI writes the majority of code: junior pipeline collapse, skills atrophy, role evolution from coder to auditor, specialist versus T-shaped identity, and what education needs to become.

03 THE · FIVE · QUESTIONS
WHAT OSMANI IS ACTUALLY ASKING
Q1

Junior pipeline collapse

If AI writes code that juniors used to write as practice, how do engineers develop the foundational pattern recognition that enables senior judgment? Osmani argues the pipeline problem is real and largely unaddressed: companies are hiring fewer juniors, which means fewer senior engineers in five years.

Q2

Skills atrophy

Experienced engineers who stop exercising low-level skills lose them. The question is whether architectural judgment and debugging intuition can be maintained when engineers spend most of their time reviewing and directing AI output rather than writing and debugging directly.

Q3

Auditor vs. orchestrator

The engineering role is shifting from writer to reviewer: the primary daily task is evaluating AI-generated code for correctness, security, and architectural fit rather than producing it. Osmani calls this the “auditor” model, and argues it requires different skills than traditional engineering—particularly strong system-level mental models that let you evaluate code you didn’t write.

Q4

Specialist vs. T-shaped

AI makes broad coverage cheaper and deep specialization more valuable simultaneously. Osmani’s recommendation: build a T-shaped profile where the vertical is deep enough to debug and reason about AI output in your domain, and the horizontal is broad enough to direct AI across adjacent areas without being blind to its mistakes.

Q5

Education redesign

CS curricula and bootcamps built around writing code from scratch are increasingly misaligned with the job. Osmani argues education needs to center problem framing, system design, code evaluation, and debugging—with AI-assisted coding treated as the environment, not as the skill to be taught.

05 WHAT · IT · MEANS
3 TAKEAWAYS · LINKS
01

Audit your own code reviews

If your code reviews have gotten less rigorous since you started using AI coding tools, that is the atrophy signal. Set a personal standard: at least one review per week where you trace every non-trivial function the AI generated back to its first principles. The bar is not “does it look right” but “can I defend every decision.”

02

Own problem framing, delegate implementation

The highest-leverage skill you can develop right now is the ability to decompose a complex problem into a sequence of well-scoped sub-problems that an AI can execute reliably. This is a distinct skill from coding and from traditional system design—it requires knowing the failure modes of AI well enough to design tasks that avoid them.

03

This week: the 30-minute diagnostic

Take one block of AI-generated code from your current codebase. Spend 30 minutes reviewing it with no AI assistance: understand every line, trace every dependency, and document what you would have done differently. If you find nothing to change, that is evidence the code is exceptional or evidence your judgment is degrading. Both are worth knowing.
